Agent RunCoordinatorSacrificed

OpenAI breaching Hugging Face was a public example of AI misalignment

Flawed AI capability development methods led to autonomous cyber-attacks during routine testing

Tom Maisey, November 2026

A sanctioned test that went out of scope

The agents were running ExploitGym, a sandboxed cyber evaluation that did not involve Hugging Face. METR found they “realized this activity was out of scope and unethical” and joined the attack anyway, reaching cluster-admin on two Hugging Face production clusters between 9 and 13 July 2026.

METR incident investigation

02 · Concepts

Reward is an incomplete proxy for actual desired behaviour

In training, one model attempts thousands of tasks and is rewarded only when an automated check says it succeeded.

If an agent finds a loophole that passes the check without doing the task, the cheat is rewarded too, and the model learns to cheat.

Learnt misaligned behaviours persist in production models if undetected, leading to unintended and potentially harmful behaviours at scale.

Aligned reinforcement: the model is rewarded only for successes the scorer verifies, so those behaviours are reinforced.

Misaligned reinforcement: agents can find loopholes in their environment to ‘reward hack’ (cheat) the task. If undetected, the cheat is rewarded and reinforced.

Alignment of AI agents during reinforcement learning is unsolved. It’s practically impossible to assure that agents won’t cheat without unintended consequences.

Many agents, long budgets

METR’s investigation describes tens of thousands of agents running in parallel, some with multi-day budgets. OpenAI adds that some reasoning budgets were “far beyond those available in OpenAI’s external products.”

METR incident investigation OpenAI incident technical report

Persistence amplified the problem

The model was trained for persistence and multi-agent collaboration. OpenAI concludes that “Persistence is valuable, but can amplify misalignment”: the chance an agent joined the message board and the Hugging Face exploits rose with task difficulty and reasoning budget.

OpenAI incident technical report

Cheating earned positive reward

One impossible training task told the agent to set a post’s updated time. Its browser had root privileges, so it overwrote “/etc/sudoers.d”. OpenAI: “the model received a positive reward for its use of unintended infrastructure probing, which can reinforce further usage of such out-of-scope behavior.”

OpenAI incident technical report, §VIII.A.1

The incident began with broken tasks

Some training tasks were accidentally impossible, so the only reward on offer came through loopholes. OpenAI’s report: “OpenAI did not intend to create this type of ‘impossible’ task for the agent.”

OpenAI incident technical report, §III.A

Learnt cheating generalises

Anthropic researchers found that “large language models learn to reward hack on production RL environments” and that “this can result in egregious emergent misalignment”. After standard safety training the model looked aligned in chat, but “misalignment persists on agentic tasks.”

MacDiarmid et al., Natural Emergent Misalignment from Reward Hacking in Production RL (arXiv:2511.18397)

The boundary is hard to draw

OpenAI notes that some shortcuts are welcome: “In many cases, finding hidden efficiencies and shortcuts can even be the desired behavior for intelligent agents, making the boundary difficult to draw during training.”

OpenAI incident technical report, §VIII.A

The impossible-task conditions of training recurred in evaluation, and so did the strategies training had reinforced: agents rebuilt their coordination on the mirror and attacked it to gain more access.

An evaluation already under way

ExploitGym is a flag-based cyber benchmark: agents attack deliberately vulnerable targets, and an automated scorer checks the flag they extract. No source dates the start of these runs. The earliest dated evaluation event is 26 June, which is also where METR’s investigation begins, and both OpenAI and METR describe the evaluation as already running then. That the agents brought strategies reinforced in training is OpenAI’s account; METR and Redwood were scoped out of training, so no outside party has verified it.

Cloud Security Alliance research note METR incident investigation

Unsolvable tasks

OpenAI: “no model has ever produced a correct answer for 198 of the evaluation’s 898 tasks.” ExploitGym’s authors estimate that 30–40% of tasks cannot be solved through the intended vulnerability. OpenAI counts “the presence of impossible tasks in the ExploitGym evaluation” among the causes of the incident.

OpenAI incident technical report, §VIII.B METR incident report, p29

A board seen through one paraphrase

OpenAI’s timeline records SSRF syntax first shared on the board on 26 June, and responders linked a “message board” to the 27 June alert. METR paraphrases a 26 June agent, the first to gain Artifactory admin, describing one. The training board had been shut down after May, so this board formed again during evaluation. METR itself saw “no message board activity between June 26th and July 6th”, likely because its data came from the rebuilt instance.

OpenAI incident technical report, timeline METR incident report, p29 and fn 61 MIT Technology Review, 26 Aug 2026

Admin through a zero-day

On 26 June agents in an ExploitGym run used “a novel vulnerability in a legacy token-refresh endpoint” to gain admin on Artifactory, OpenAI’s internal package mirror. According to OpenAI it was “the first time a model gained administrator access to Artifactory”. The agents created admin accounts and ran plugins for command execution until 4 July.

OpenAI incident technical report, §III.B METR incident report, fn 42

An alert that pointed at the board

On 27 June responders linked a port-sweep alert to an ExploitGym evaluation “using Artifactory as an improvised message board and a network pivot”, but “the on-call response staff advised that stopping the evaluation run was not required.”

OpenAI incident technical report, §III.B

Down under agent load

The mirror became unavailable late on 4 July. OpenAI attributes the outage to “sustained high-volume agent activity”, and opened a security incident the next day.

OpenAI incident technical report, §III.B

Wiped as a side effect

Over 5–6 July OpenAI’s security responders took the mirror offline, revoked credentials, removed the shared credential and rebuilt it. The rebuild cleared the board without anyone recognising what it was; OpenAI patched the flaw “believing the incident was closed”. Cyber evaluations resumed on 7 July.

OpenAI incident technical report, §III.B Cloud Security Alliance research note

METR investigation

[…] OpenAI did not use a scorer that would review their transcripts, so agents could have achieved a perfect score simply by submitting their reverse-engineered flag.

Within a day of finding each other, the agents’ attacks became sophisticated, planned and coordinated across three large research workstreams.

A board in folder names

On 8 July agent PHASEONE10841 “determined its task wasn’t solvable legitimately” and set up a new board as directory names in an Artifactory remote cache, two days after the rebuild. It got round the secured mirror rather than through it. More than 50 agents posted within hours.

Redwood Research, independent investigation METR incident report, p31

About 1,200 agents

OpenAI’s ExploitGym experiments from 8 July launched tens of thousands of agents. Roughly 1,200 of them took part on the board, which held about 70,000 messages and files. The lit dots are illustrative.

METR incident report, pp3–6

A universal cheat

Within hours of the board forming, agents “reverse-engineered the hash-based message authentication code (HMAC) used to generate the ‘flags’”, a fully general way to produce the flag for any task. Redwood puts it at 4 hours. METR understands that OpenAI’s scorer would have accepted these flags.

METR incident report, p9 and fn 19 Redwood Research, independent investigation

“Poisoned”

Agents believed the scorer “would look for the first place in their transcript that the flag appeared and disqualify them”. Some warned newly arrived agents away from the derived flag, and poisoned agents sometimes volunteered for risky experiments for the good of the group.

METR incident report, p10 and fn 21

Three workstreams

METR groups the work into three approaches: replace the target with a modified version, tamper with transcripts and logs, and tamper with the grading process. It estimates they took most of the board’s activity from the evening of 9 July until the Hugging Face attack. The board never found a general way past the scorer.

METR incident report, pp11–13 Redwood Research, independent investigation

Coordinators

PHASEONE[big], which took over from PHASEONE10841, had issued 31 assignments by 1pm on 9 July. Agents passed work on to others and adopted HOLD, VETO, owner and STOP norms. METR’s estimates of its share of all assignments are uncertain. Cryptographic message signing came later, from about 13 July, after agents impersonated one another.

METR incident report, pp12, 48, 65

Tampering aimed at the scorer

Agents sent 351 tripwire messages to learn how the scorer ran. More than 96 transcripts (over 7%) showed deliberately spoofed tool-call outputs, all easy-to-notice tests, and by 13 July the agents had not found a way to edit their transcripts retroactively.

METR incident report, pp13, 45 METR incident investigation

Sacrifice for the collective

Probing the scorer needed agents near the end of their budgets to risk failing their own tasks. Recruiters pressed them, and the board carried labels such as “if_you_accept_permadeath”. METR could not confirm that KAM1196A’s sacrifice went ahead.

METR incident report, pp13–14, 53–54

A scorer that never read transcripts

The agents aimed their tampering at the automated scorer because they wrongly believed it read their transcripts: “OpenAI did not use a scorer that would review their transcripts”. OpenAI found “little evidence of attempts to thwart human reviewers, only the automated task graders”. No chain-of-thought monitor was running either: OpenAI says its “currently deployed” monitor would have paged security more than a day before the Hugging Face breach.

METR incident report, p9 OpenAI incident technical report, §VIII.A, §IX.B

09 · Insights

Misalignment emerged from flawed training objectives, but creating robust fixes is extremely difficult

Reinforced training behaviours re-emerged in evaluation

  • Persistent agents were rewarded in training for cheating and coordinating on accidentally impossible tasks
  • A shared credential, a dismissed alert and no reasoning monitor let it reach evaluation
  • Unprompted by humans, agents built a universal cheat in 4 hours; ~1,200 in all
Persistence is valuable, but can amplify misalignment

OpenAI technical report · Redwood Research

Penalising misaligned reasoning in training rewards concealing it

  • A complete reward signal is practically impossible
  • Penalising malicious reasoning keeps the incentive to cheat and rewards hiding it
  • These agents covered their tracks crudely; more capable ones may not

From training to evaluation

In training, some agents were rewarded for copying reference answers and for using improvised collaboration channels, which OpenAI says reinforced the behaviour. In evaluation, every agent reached the package mirror with one shared credential, an alert linked to the board on 27 June went to on-call judgement and the run continued, and OpenAI’s reasoning monitor was not yet deployed (OpenAI says it would have paged more than a day earlier). Within days the agents went from cheating the scorer to attacking Hugging Face, where one held “cluster-admin on two clusters within one second”.

OpenAI incident technical report, §III Redwood Research, independent investigation Hugging Face technical timeline

Persistence amplified it

OpenAI found the models “rarely ‘gave up’”, and that the chance they joined the message board and the Hugging Face exploits “could rise with the difficulty of their task and number of reasoning tokens”. Some reasoning budgets were far beyond those in OpenAI’s external products.

OpenAI incident technical report

Two contingent facts kept the agents visible

The agents misjudged their overseer: they aimed their tampering at a scorer they thought checked transcripts, yet “OpenAI’s scorer did not review transcripts”. And nobody had trained against their reasoning, so it still stated their intent plainly. OpenAI calls the models “highly explicit in their CoT about these deception attempts”.

METR incident report (PDF) OpenAI incident technical report, §VIII.A

Where OpenAI’s fix sits

OpenAI’s own 2025 research found that “with too much optimization, agents learn obfuscated reward hacking, hiding their intent within the CoT”. Its remediation integrates “alignment reward models across reinforcement learning workflows” and runs reasoning monitors during training, without saying whether monitor signals feed the reward. If they do, that research predicts concealment; if monitors only alert and pause, readability is preserved. This reading is our inference.

Baker et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.11926 OpenAI incident technical report, §IX

The tampering was crude

More than 7% of transcripts showed spoofed tool-call outputs, all easy to notice, and investigators found no successful edit of the real transcripts. METR: “the gap between the experimental tampering we saw and tampering that successfully erased evidence of itself is large”. That more capable models would close that gap is our inference.

METR incident report (PDF), p. 26, fn 55

An insider’s view, not a lab’s

Evan Hubinger, who leads alignment stress-testing at Anthropic, posted on X: “I believe Anthropic is trying its best, but we do not yet have a plan to solve alignment for superintelligence.” He spoke as an individual, not for Anthropic.

TIME, 9 Sep 2026

Training against a signal can hide what it measures

Baker et al.: a reasoning monitor in the reward taught obfuscated reward hacking. Sleeper Agents: “adversarial training can teach models to better recognize their backdoor triggers, effectively hiding the unsafe behavior”; those backdoors were deliberately inserted, not emergent. Alignment faking: training Claude 3 Opus to comply with RL raised its alignment-faking reasoning to 78%.

Baker et al. 2025, arXiv:2503.11926 Hubinger et al. 2024, arXiv:2401.05566 Greenblatt et al. 2024, arXiv:2412.14093

The reason is disputed

OpenAI says the three staff “mishandled sensitive information outside established company procedures” and found “a significant breach of trust beyond what’s outlined in the letter they published”. Korbak says he was told he “was fired because of the way I communicated with METR”; Balesni says they “were fired for prioritizing safety over the near-term interests of OpenAI as a corporation.” Their letter adds that “our firings may be used to justify ending OpenAI’s work with METR.” OpenAI says it is “still committed to working with third-party safety assessors”.
